## Madeleine

My mother is impossible to shop for. She is the type of person who, if she wants something, has usually already bought it herself. Over the years, we've settled into a different tradition: instead of trying to find her a birthday or Christmas present, I help her business with whatever problem is causing her the most grief at the time. Some years that has meant untangling her IT, whether setting up a new computer or helping her migrate off cable to YouTube TV. Last year, it meant doing hours of cake deliveries around New York City (I even drove a cake all the way to Boston once). For her most recent birthday, I told her that my gift would be seeing whether any of the AI tools I had spent the last two years learning about at Stanford could actually make her life easier.

To understand why there was so much opportunity, it helps to understand both my mom and her business. While she decorated her first cake at eight years old, baking remained more of a passion than a profession for most of her life. Instead, she built a career in magazines, spending three decades styling the pages of *House Beautiful*, *Real Simple*, and *Woman's Day*, while also contributing regularly to *The Today Show*. In hindsight, the transition feels obvious. The same eye for composition, color, and storytelling that shaped magazine spreads eventually found its way onto cakes. During the pandemic, she began baking for neighbors in Millbrook, New York, and what started as a creative outlet became a business. She named it The Dutchy of Millbrook, a name borrowed from *The Cazalet Chronicles*, with a "t" added as a wink to Dutchess County.

She has amassed more than 60,000 organic Instagram followers (Exhibit 1) and produces 10–15 custom cakes a week, often more during busy periods. Her signature product is the charm cake: a custom portrait rendered in buttercream, where each small illustration represents a person, pet, place, or passion in the recipient's life. The result is part celebration cake, part visual biography.

*Exhibit 1: Elizabeth Mayhew Instagram*

What amazed me was that despite the scale of the business, many of the systems behind it looked almost exactly as they had when she was baking for neighbors. Customers reach out however they feel like it: Instagram DM, email, text message, sometimes even through a friend of a friend. My mother manually sends pricing information, answers the same questions over and over again, schedules phone calls to gather details, and, if an order is confirmed, records it in the Notes app on her phone (Exhibit 2).

*Exhibit 2: Elizabeth Mayhew Notes App*

The business works largely because my mother is extraordinarily talented and somehow keeps the entire operation in her head. It is also, from a systems perspective, complete chaos. That chaos became the starting point for this project.

### Artifact 1: "Charm Cake Studio"

The first pain point I went after was the one my mother mentioned most often, and the more I looked at it, the more I realized it was hurting both sides of the transaction at once.

From my mother's side, the problem was time. Before she can begin a charm cake, she has to know what the customer wants on it — which people, pets, places, and passions, and roughly how they should be arranged. Because customers can rarely articulate this clearly, she ends up sketching ideas, talking them through what's possible, and going back and forth until a picture emerges, one cake at a time. At ten to fifteen cakes a week, that pre-production conversation is a real and recurring tax on her time. And it often ends badly: customers arrive having fallen in love with something that simply cannot be made — a paragraph of script lettering that would never be legible in buttercream, or two dozen intricate charms crammed onto an eight-inch cake. Once someone is attached to an impossible design, my mother's job quietly gets harder, because now she has to talk them out of a picture they already love without dampening their excitement.

From the customer's side, the problem was uncertainty. A charm cake starts at $650 and is entirely bespoke, which means people are committing real money to something they cannot see in advance. They have no intuition for how many charms will fit, what will read clearly, or how it will all come together — and no good way to communicate the vision in their head through an Instagram DM. The gap between what they imagine and what they can describe is exactly where the nervous back-and-forth lives.

My first instinct was the obvious one: build a tool that generates an image of the customer's dream cake. But the more I sat with it, the clearer it became that an open-ended image generator would work against my mother, not for her. It would happily produce gorgeous, completely unbakeable cakes — and every one of them would become a customer she had to disappoint. That led to the most important design decision of the entire project: the Charm Cake Studio would be a *constrained* design surface, not a blank canvas. It would let customers be creative, but only within the boundaries of what my mother can actually make. A tool that simply said "yes" to anything would actively make her job harder — and that realization, that usefulness can come from constraint rather than capability, shaped everything I built afterward.

The studio starts customers with my mother's real building blocks (Exhibit 3): her scalloped frame — the linked-arc border she describes as "smiles linked together" — in her actual palette of buttercream colors. They add an inscription, choose its color, and place charms they can drag, resize, and rotate directly on the cake, exactly the way she arranges them by hand. The hardest design problem was the charm library itself, because a charm cake can feature almost anything — a tennis racket, a specific breed of dog, a school crest, a passion flower. I couldn't hand-draw an infinite catalog, but I also couldn't leave customers staring at a blank box, so I layered it: a curated set of her most common charms leads, a much larger library fills in behind it for specific searches, and for the truly particular — a beloved pet, a company logo — a customer can upload a photo and drop it on like any other charm. The point was never the plumbing; it was that the tool almost never dead-ends, yet always shows my mother's on-brand options first — gently steering taste without ever blocking it.

What made those choices right was never my own judgment. It was my mother's, delivered in real time. I would build a version, show it to her, and watch her react in seconds — the same way she reads a finished cake. "The writing's too small." "Those strawberries are perfect, but that phrase won't fit beside them." "You'd never put that there." She could not have written me a specification for any of it; the knowledge lives in her hands and her eye, not in sentences. But she could look at a wrong version and tell me exactly why it was wrong, instantly — and my job was to catch each reaction and set it down before it evaporated. That, far more than any line of code, is what the studio was actually made of.

Building it also taught me an early lesson about the difference between a demo and a product. One version worked perfectly on my laptop but exposed credentials that should never have been visible to users. Fixing it was simple once I understood the issue, but it drove home that "works" and "ready for strangers" are different standards, and that much of real software lives in the gap between them.

The deeper takeaway, though, was that the hard part of this build had almost nothing to do with generating images. It was understanding my mother's expertise well enough to decide which parts to turn into firm rules, which into gentle defaults, and which to leave to her. And solving it surfaced the next problem immediately: I had given customers a beautiful way to dream up a cake, and then nowhere to actually go with it.

### Artifact 2: The Dutchy Website

Solving the visualization problem made the next bottleneck obvious. A customer could now imagine their cake, but they still had to reach my mother the same chaotic way everyone did: an Instagram DM, an email, a text, or a message passed through a mutual friend. Every one of those threads landed in a different place, and every one started the same way — with my mother manually sending pricing, answering the same handful of questions she had answered a hundred times, and scheduling a call to extract the details she needed. The Charm Cake Studio had made the *front* of the experience better while leaving the actual intake exactly as scattered as before. So I used Claude Code to build her a real front door: a full website for The Dutchy of Millbrook (Exhibit 4).

The site does several jobs my mother had been doing by hand. A portfolio gathers more than a hundred of her cakes into one place, loading images only as a visitor scrolls and letting any photo open full-size, so customers can browse her range without her digging up examples in DMs. An FAQ answers, once and for good, the questions she had been fielding individually for years — the exact pricing tiers, the flavors and fillings she offers, the fact that she'll bake almost anything except funfetti and red velvet, that the outside is always vanilla French buttercream, and that a cake must be refrigerated and rested before serving. Simply moving that information onto a page reclaimed a meaningful slice of her week.

The heart of the site is the custom order flow, and it is where the studio's lesson paid off. Rather than a generic contact form, I built an intake that adapts to what someone is ordering (Exhibit 5). Choosing a cake type opens a tailored set of questions and hides the rest, so a wedding inquiry is never cluttered with charm-placement questions and a charm inquiry isn't asked about tiers. A charm-cake order surfaces my mother's real size-and-price tiers and, crucially, a charm list where the customer adds their charms and then drags them into order of importance — because that priority is information my mother genuinely needs. She fits on as many charms as the cake can hold, working down the list, so the order of the list is not decoration; it is instruction. In other words, the form doesn't just collect data. It collects my mother's expertise *from the customer*, asking the questions she would have asked on the phone, in the order she would have asked them.

This is where I learned that encoding expertise is not a one-time act. The same knowledge I had built into the studio — what makes a cake feasible, what a baker must know before she can say yes — had to be expressed all over again, completely differently, in the structure of a form. Good products don't merely display expertise; they shape the interaction so the right information surfaces by default.

But the website also revealed its own limitation, and naming it plainly is the hinge into my final project. At first, when a customer submitted that beautifully structured form, the site simply turned everything — the answers, the ranked charm list — into an email and sent it to my mother. This was the right call for a one-person business with no appetite for new logins, and it shipped, which counts. But it meant the website was excellent at *capturing* a well-structured inquiry and did nothing to help *manage* it once it landed; each new inquiry just joined the same pile of messages she was already drowning in. The intake had gotten dramatically better; the handling of it had not changed at all. In solving the intake problem, I had accidentally exposed an operations problem — the very one sitting in my mother's Notes app this whole time.

### Artifact 3: Order Management System

By the time I had built the studio and the website, the real shape of my mother's problem had finally come into focus. The front of her business now looked polished, but the back of it still lived in a single, ever-growing note on her phone (Exhibit 2): confirmed orders, due dates, charm lists, flavors, pickup details, and who had paid, all in one undifferentiated scroll. There was no way to see what was due this week, no way to tell at a glance which orders were confirmed versus still in conversation, and no record that would survive if the note were ever lost. The business ran on my mother's memory, and her memory was the single point of failure.

So the final build was an Order Book (Exhibit 6): a private, password-protected system that replaces the note with an actual database. Where the first two artifacts were customer-facing, this one is purely operational — it is the back office my mother never had. Every order is a structured record rather than a line of text: the customer's name and contact, the cake type, the occasion and date it's needed, the full order details, and the money — total, deposit, balance, and whether it has been paid. She can look at the same orders three ways: a board that shows each cake moving through the stages of her week — Inquiry, Confirmed, Baking, Ready, Delivered — so the whole pipeline is visible at a glance; a sortable table for scanning or editing everything at once; and a history of completed orders that tallies what she has actually collected. A running line keeps the numbers she cares about in front of her — how many orders are active, and how much money is still outstanding — and because it is backed by a real database rather than a file on a single phone, it syncs live across her devices and survives a lost phone, a closed laptop, or a dropped call.

Building the Order Book also let me close the loop I had left open with the website. The intake form no longer just emails my mother; it now writes each new inquiry straight into the Order Book, where it appears automatically as a new order at the Inquiry stage. The three artifacts had quietly become one system: a customer dreams up a cake in the studio, submits it through the site, and lands — structured, detailed, and trackable — in the very place my mother runs her week.

What strikes me, looking at it, is how little of the difficulty was technical. Standing up a database, a login, and a live-updating interface — the kind of thing that would have meant hiring an engineer a few years ago — took an evening with Claude Code. The genuinely hard part was the same as it had been all along: deciding what my mother actually needed to see. The first two artifacts had encoded her craft, her sense of what is feasible and what is beautiful. This one had to encode her operations — the path an order takes from a DM to a finished cake going out the door — which meant learning a different kind of expertise from her: the kind that lives in how she organizes her week, not in how she holds a piping bag.

It is also the newest of the three, and what's left to do is no longer really a feature; it's adoption. A note on her phone, for all its chaos, has zero friction, and the hardest part of any internal tool is convincing the person it was built for to leave the messy system they trust for the tidy one they don't yet. Getting my mother to actually live in the Order Book — to let it replace the note rather than sit beside it — is the real work that remains, and it has almost nothing to do with code.

### What I Learned

None of these projects would have been feasible for me even six months earlier. Claude Code let me move from idea to working prototype at a speed that would have been unimaginable in my old life as a product manager at Disney, where a new feature meant finding an engineer, writing requirements, waiting through a sprint cycle, and negotiating tradeoffs. Now the same thing could be built, tested, and revised in an evening. The technology genuinely changed what was possible. What surprised me was that it did not change what was difficult.

That distinction is the most interesting answer I found to the question behind this independent study. Building with AI makes implementation so cheap that problem selection starts to dominate everything else. Before these tools, someone like me — with no engineering background — might have spent months building the wrong thing. Now I can build the wrong thing in a weekend. That sounds like a failure mode, and it is precisely the point: the faster I got at building, the more the binding question stopped being "can I build this?" and became "is this the right thing to build next?" The bottleneck moved from execution to judgment.

Which is why, looking back, this project was far less about learning to code than about learning to translate expertise. My mother can look at a cake design for two seconds and know whether it will work. Over the course of this project I kept finding myself trying to turn that glance into specifications — first in a visualizer, then in an intake flow, and finally in an operational system. The technology changed from project to project; the challenge never did. The AI handled the building. Learning what to build — and how to set my mother's judgment down so that a stranger could use it well — was the actual education, and it is the part I will keep.
