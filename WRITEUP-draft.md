## Madeleine

Every year, my Christmas present to my mother is the same in spirit but different in form: I help her small business with whatever she needs that year. Some years that has meant untangling her IT, some years sorting out a delivery problem. My mother, Elizabeth Mayhew, is a baker — though that undersells it. After thirty years styling the pages of *House Beautiful*, *Real Simple*, and *Woman's Day*, and years as a contributor to *The Today Show*, she decorated her first cake at eight years old and never really stopped. When the world went quiet during the pandemic, she began baking for her isolating neighbors in Millbrook, New York, and what started as comfort became a calling. She named it *The Dutchy of Millbrook* — a name borrowed from *The Cazalet Chronicles*, with a "t" added as a wink to Dutchess County. Her signature is the charm cake: a custom portrait rendered in buttercream, where each small drawn charm stands for a person, a pet, a place, or a passion in the recipient's life, the whole cake assembling into a kind of edible charm bracelet. She doesn't sketch. She starts with a scalloped frame — "they're like smiles linked together," she says — and doodles directly onto the cake, the way she once styled a room or a magazine cover.

This year, I told her my present would be my AI knowledge. I would spend the quarter using modern development tools to actually build things for her business — not slideware, not recommendations, but working software she could use. What I did not anticipate was that each project would expose the need for the next, until I had built three connected products and learned something at every step that I could not have predicted at the start. That progression is the real story of my work, and it maps almost exactly onto the question our group set out to answer: whether people without engineering backgrounds can become genuinely proficient at building real AI-powered products. For me the answer turned out to be yes. But the hard part was almost never the technology.

Before describing the builds, it is worth naming the working method that carried through all three, because it became a lesson in itself. I learned very quickly to prototype visually and disposably first — to get something on the screen I could react to with my eyes, refine it in plain-language conversation, and only then commit to building it properly. Almost every good decision I made came from seeing a wrong version first. My mother could not tell me in the abstract what made a mockup feel "off," but she could glance at a rendered cake and say "the writing's too small" or "that won't sit there" in an instant. The entire project, in a sense, was an exercise in turning her glance into specifications.

### Project 1: The Charm Cake Visualizer

The project began before the quarter officially started, around Christmas, when my mother mentioned two recurring frustrations. First, she spent significant time making mockups so that customers could picture a finished cake before committing. Second, customers struggled to understand what was actually feasible in buttercream. At first glance this seemed like an ideal AI problem: why not build a tool that let customers design their own charm cakes and generate a visualization before they ordered?

As I dug in, I realized the challenge was not generating images. The real difficulty was translating domain expertise into software. My mother carries years of tacit knowledge. She knows text has to be relatively large to stay legible in buttercream. She knows intricate decorations devour valuable space. She knows a small strawberry works beautifully, while a long phrase in script lettering might demand an entirely different cake size. Customers possess none of this. Left unconstrained — handed, say, a blank canvas and an image generator — they design things that look lovely on a screen and are difficult or impossible to execute in reality. A tool that simply said "yes" to anything would actively make my mother's job harder, because she would then have to walk the customer back from a picture they had already fallen in love with.

So I made a deliberate design decision that shaped everything after it: the visualizer would be a *constrained* design surface, not an open-ended image generator. It would let people be creative inside the boundaries of what my mother can actually make, rather than outside them. Branded as the "Charm Cake Studio," the tool opens onto a cake stage carrying her real signature elements (Exhibit 1). The scalloped frame she describes as "smiles linked together" is rendered as an actual geometric edge — generated as a path of twenty-six small linked arcs around the cake — and offered in her real working palette of buttercream colors, plus a free-choice color picker. A customer types an inscription, chooses its color, and adds it to the cake. Then they add charms, which they can drag to reposition, resize, and rotate freely, each charm carrying small on-cake handles for those actions. Because I was still learning what the product needed to be, I optimized for speed rather than permanence: the visualizer was something I could change quickly, show my mother, and revise again the same afternoon.

The most interesting design problem was the charm library, and it is where the "constrained but not limiting" philosophy got tested. A charm cake can feature almost anything — a tennis racket, a particular breed of dog, a school crest, a passion flower — so I could not hand-draw an infinite catalog, but I also could not leave customers staring at a blank box. My solution was to layer it: a hand-curated palette of the most common charms leads, a much larger icon library fills in behind it for more specific searches, and for the truly particular — a beloved pet, a company logo — a customer can upload a photo and drop it onto the cake like any other charm. The point was never the plumbing; it was that the tool almost never dead-ends, yet it always shows my mother's on-brand charms first — gently steering taste without ever blocking it.

What made those decisions right was never my own judgment. It was my mother's, delivered in real time. I would build a version, show it to her, and watch her react in seconds — the same way she reads a finished cake. "The writing's too small." "Those strawberries are perfect, but that phrase won't fit beside them." "You'd never put that there." She could not have written me a specification for any of it; the knowledge lives in her hands and her eye, not in sentences. But she could look at a wrong version and tell me precisely why it was wrong, instantly — and my job was to catch each reaction and set it down before it evaporated. That, far more than any line of code, is what the visualizer was actually made of.

I also learned an early lesson about the difference between a demo and a product. One version of the visualizer worked perfectly on my laptop but exposed credentials that should never have been visible to users. Fixing it was straightforward once I understood the issue, but it taught me that "works" and "ready for strangers" are different standards — and that much of real software development lives in that gap.

Building the visualizer forced me past image generation and into product design. The goal was never to make pictures; it was to build a system that nudged users toward realistic outcomes while preserving their creativity, and that quietly carried my mother's judgment inside it — the legible-text rule, the spatial sense of what fits, the house style of the scalloped edge. I was, in effect, trying to encode a baker's judgment into software. That was my first and most durable lesson of the quarter: the hardest part of building an AI product is rarely the technology. It is understanding the underlying expertise the product is supposed to capture, and then deciding which parts of it to make into rules, which into gentle defaults, and which to leave to the human.

### Project 2: The Website

The visualizer solved one problem and immediately revealed a larger one. While building it, I kept asking my mother how customers actually reached her, and the answer was always the same: Instagram. Every inquiry, every exchange about size and flavor and delivery date, every back-and-forth about whether a design was feasible — all of it lived in direct messages, scrolling away into an unsearchable feed. She did not just need a visualizer. She needed a front door: a real home for the business that could carry her work, set expectations, and gather the right information from customers before the conversation even started.

So I used Claude Code to build her a full website (Exhibit 2). This was a different kind of building from the visualizer — a multi-page site with a real structure rather than a single clever screen — and it was my first sustained experience of directing a coding agent through a project rather than a one-off. The site has a home page anchored by a gallery of her cakes, an about page that tells her story, a portfolio, a contact page, an FAQ, terms, and the order flow that became the heart of it. I developed a brand identity to hold it all together: a navy-and-pink palette drawn from her own cakes, the Jost and Inter typefaces, her logo, and the line that became the site's spine — "Every cake tells a story."

Two parts of the site are worth describing in detail, because they are where the visualizer's lesson paid off. The first is the portfolio, which sounds trivial and was quietly instructive. My mother had over a hundred cake photos, and the naïve version — dumping all of them onto a page at full size — would have made the site painfully slow to load. So the gallery holds 102 images that load lazily, only as the visitor scrolls to them, and any photo can be clicked to open full-size in a lightbox. It taught me that even "just show the pictures" has real engineering underneath it once you care about whether the thing is actually usable.

The second, and the centerpiece, is the custom order flow. Rather than a generic contact form, I built an intake that adapts to what someone is ordering. The page presents the cake types as cards; choosing one opens a tailored inquiry, and behind the scenes the form shows only the question groups relevant to that choice and disables the rest, so a wedding inquiry is never accidentally asked about charm placement and the hidden fields never quietly sneak into what gets sent. A charm-cake inquiry surfaces her real size-and-price tiers and, crucially, a charm list where customers add their charms and then drag them into priority order — encoding my mother's actual working reality that she fits on as many charms as the cake can hold, working down the list, so the order of the list is information she needs, not decoration. A wedding inquiry instead asks about guest count, tiers, venue, and design vision; lattice, trellis, and "something else" each carry their own tailored fields, before everything funnels into shared questions about flavor, filling, finish, pickup, and delivery. The FAQ, similarly, is not generic filler — it encodes her real policies, from the exact pricing tiers to the fact that she'll bake almost anything except funfetti and red velvet, that the outside is always vanilla French buttercream, and that the cake must be refrigerated and rested an hour before serving.

The lesson here built directly on the first. Encoding expertise is not a one-time act performed inside a single product. The same knowledge — what makes a cake feasible, what a baker must know before she can say yes — had to be expressed again, and completely differently, in the architecture of a form than it had been in a drag-and-drop canvas. Good products do not merely display expertise; they shape the interaction so that the right information surfaces by default, and they are honest about where they stop.

The custom order flow is also where I ran into the most honest limitation of the project, and I want to name it plainly because it is the hinge into my third build. When a customer submits that carefully structured form, the site does not send the information to a database or a dashboard. It assembles everything — the grouped answers, the ranked charm list rendered out in order of importance, the names of any photos to attach — into a pre-filled email addressed to my mother, and opens it for the customer to send. It is, by design, "a request, not a confirmed order." This was the right call for a one-person business with no appetite for new logins, and it shipped, which counts. But it means the website is superb at *capturing* a well-structured inquiry and does nothing at all to help *manage* what happens after it lands in her inbox. The intake got dramatically better; the handling of it did not change at all. In solving the intake problem, I had accidentally exposed an operations problem.

### What the arc adds up to

Three builds, each one summoned by the last. A visualizer that taught me the hard part is capturing expertise, not generating images — and that a tool which says "yes" to everything can be less useful than one that knows how to say "not like that." A website that taught me the same expertise has to be re-encoded into every new surface, and that solving one problem cleanly tends to expose the next one beneath it. And an order management tool that [final lesson, to be written with that section].

None of these projects would have been feasible for me even six months earlier. Claude Code let me move from idea to working prototype at a speed that would have been unimaginable in my old life as a product manager at Disney, where a new feature meant finding an engineer, writing requirements, waiting through a sprint cycle, and negotiating tradeoffs. Now the same thing could be built, tested, and revised in an evening. The technology genuinely changed what was possible. What surprised me was that it did not change what was difficult.

The deeper lesson sits underneath all three, and it is the one I would point our whole group toward. Building with AI makes implementation so cheap that problem selection starts to dominate everything else. Before these tools, someone like me — with no engineering background — might have spent months building the wrong thing. Now I can build the wrong thing in a weekend. That sounds like a failure mode, and it is precisely the point: the faster I got at building, the more the binding question stopped being "can I build this?" and became "is this the right thing to build next?" The bottleneck moved from execution to judgment.

Which is why, looking back, the project was far less about learning to code than about learning to translate expertise. My mother can look at a cake design for two seconds and know whether it will work. Over the course of the quarter I kept finding myself trying to turn that glance into specifications — first in a visualizer, then in an intake flow, and finally in an operational system. The technology changed from project to project; the challenge never did. The AI handled the building. Learning what to build — and how to set my mother's judgment down so that a stranger could use it well — was the actual education, and it is the part I will keep.
